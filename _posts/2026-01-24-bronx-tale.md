**A Bronx Tale**

So, it was around Christmas and I’d just lucked into the AMC+ discount on prime. Mainly got it for some fantastic period dramas (lookin at you Halt and Catch Fire \- great show, terrible name), but there was a superb catalogue of holiday films available. Headlining the list was A Bronx Tale, which is one of those movies I’d always heard referenced but never seen. I mainly knew it from the “Great Ones” monologue, which has been recut in several rap songs (most prominently on Anderson .Paak’s “Winners Circle”). Figured it was as good a time as any to watch it, and it was honestly solid. Let’s get into it.

**Setting:**   
The movie starts off with scenic shots of a snowy NYC with a barbershop quartet hitting the high notes, which I thought was a really nice 90s-2000s kids intro to the Christmas setting. After this we get to the Bronx, where Calogero’s a nine-year old growing up in the heart of an Italian neighborhood who’s gonna be our main character and monologuing narrator for the rest of the movie. His father Lorenzo is a blue-collar, salt of the Earth bus driver, and he takes Calogero through the city on his route, establishing him as the link to the rest of the city’s working class. After Calogero gets dropped off, he ostensibly heads home, but sneaks into the bar next door through the back. Here we start to meet the colorful mafioso types, grown ups with a balance of humor, intensity, and responsibility.  We’re introduced to Sonny, the head honcho, whose MO is to protect his people by any means.

And that’s mostly it for the settings. Sure, we get a few cutaways to school and other neighborhoods for scenes, but the bar, the house, and the street are pretty much where Calogero grows up. He’s got a couple of idiot friends here, a love interest there, but the story is about these three men, particularly about how Calogero finds who he is between Lorenzo and Sonny. We get to see both the summer and the winter, and since it’s working class NYC we get a lot of mileage out of the characters walking everywhere, with shots to restaurants, stick ball, street side homicides, bicycle gangs, police line ups, and racial tensions. 

**Characters:**   
This is a story about Calogero, Lorenzo, and Sonny, with Calogero as the narrative glue while Lorenzo (Robert De Niro in his directorial debut) and Sonny (Chazz Palmintieri, original writer of the play, in his acting debut) represent the real dramatic heavyweights. Calogero is played both as a precocious 9 year-old and an emotionally developing 17 year old. The decision for a dual role is thoughtful, as young Calogero is whip-smart and street savvy, presenting as an easy character to root for and stay interested in. The writing gives him some very funny dialogue and helps understand the seeds of his bond with Sonny and his relationship with his father. This appeal has to do some heavy lifting to keep us on Calogero’s side later on, where he struggles to keep his sense of the right thing around his own boys’ club, who continually grow towards territoriality and racial aggression. Calogero faces this continual conflict between justice and loyalty, and his interpretation on how to act here is tempered by his experiences with Sonny.

Sonny is the main adult featured in both Calogero’s childhood and adolescence, serving as confidante, advisor, and mentor. Well, when the times are good anyways. After Calogero saves him early in the movie, he dubs him ‘C’. This sets up a nice dual identity between the C that Sonny trains in his intimate understanding of the neighborhood, and the Calogero that both Sonny and Lorenzo hope can be better than them and make it out. 

Sonny and Lorenzo are contrasted, sure, and they’re often in conflict over the influence the other is having over Calogero. Beyond that, however, they’re both really just preoccupied with survival, with differences coming from their positioning. Lorenzo, as a bus driver, has found a position to avoid the strifes and perils of the streets, and he knows his son’s best chance of survival is to keep his head down in the same way. Sonny, however, has been in the game from a young age. As dangerous as his position can be, it gives him control and responsibility over others’ survival, as well as the perks of prestige. Avoiding the conflict was an option lost to him long back, so while he would like to help Calogero grow, the only wisdom and morality he can offer him are tempered by his experience as a gangster, making it difficult to teach without romanticizing his life.

**Plot:**   
The plot itself is a) googleable and b) meh, so I’ll just cover the moments I remember. Movie starts off with the setting as described above, but things kick off when a guy parks on the street in front of Calogero’s with him staring out the window. Another guy pulls up with a baseball bat and starts smashing the windshield of the car, and pretty sure he brains the guy in the driver’s seat as well. Sonny does what all calm and rational leaders in this scenario would do, and point blank executes the guy with the bat. They dump the gun and try to go back to business as normal, but the police organize a lineup. The only witness is thought to be Calogero, and while Lorenzo tries to keep him out, Calogero gets taken down. Already sensitive at a young age to the morals he’s seen at the bar, he tells them the shooter isn’t in the lineup. The next time Sonny sees him at the bar, he calls him over and their friendship begins. 

Calogero grows up, and his father and mother keep telling him to stay out of the bar and stay on the straight and narrow. At the bar, he’s more or less Sonny’s little brother. Pretty soon, Calogero falls for an African American girl named Jane, which causes him some strife because the heart of Italian NYC isn’t exactly a melting pot, and his friends aren’t paragons of tolerance. Sonny gives him the “Great Ones” monologue, and Calogero talks to her when he sees her at school. Sonny lets Calogero borrow the car to go on a date, and Calogero tries to pick her up but gets chastised because her brother recognizes him as one of the guys who beat up him and his friends while they were riding bikes. Calogero, to his credit, had been trying to protect him by asking him to play dead (definitely still threw some real punches), but Calogero also immediately loses any goodwill by calling him a racial epithet.

Calogero then finds out that his boys club had eggs thrown at them after beating up the bicyclists, who naturally escalate to FIREBOMBING THE RECORD STORE. Molotov Cocktails and everything (were there literally any adults at home in the 60s?). Between the emotions of recent arguments with Sonny and Lorenzo as well as his heartbreak, he says sure, why not. Sonny pulls up to tell him exactly why not and forces him to get out of the car before taking him back to the neighborhood. Here, thankful that Sonny still came back for him, he’s surprised to find Jane, whose brother remembered and cleared him of active wrongdoing (again, still definitely used a racial epithet). 

They have a ‘yeah this movie was definitely from the 80s’ kiss before he realizes oh wait, his friends are ABOUT TO FIREBOMB A BLACK RECORD STORE. He takes Sonny’s car and drives with Jane back to the neighborhood, where they’re able to keep her brother away from the store. His friends still arrive and bury the store in liquid fire. As they begin their getaway, someone from the store finds an unexploded Molotov and throws it back at the car, which is promptly engulfed in flames. Calogero and Jane watch as his friends are identified as flaming carcasses.

Calogero drives back to find Sonny in the middle of celebration, smiling back at him in a crowded bar. The moment is too perfect, setting up for Sonny’s sudden execution at the hands of the son of the man he’d killed all those years ago. We see the funeral, where Calogero is able to see the end that Lorenzo had been all too aware of while Lorenzo sees all the good Sonny had meant for the community by the forest of flowers surrounding his casket. Joe Pesci, in the role of the man Sonny had saved all those years back, comes to the funeral, and offers Calogero a lifeline should he ever need it as Sonny did for him. Calogero and Lorenzo go home, both respecting the bigger picture of a man who they had misunderstood in different ways.

**Cinematics:**   
The movie is very well done for a directorial debut. The quartet soundtrack really pulled me in at the beginning and end of the movie. The rest of the movie was fairly sparse in terms of soundtracking, but there’s always an appropriate amount of environmental noise to contribute to the city effect and gritty feel that De Niro wants to set. The cinematics are overall secondary to the performances by De Niro and Palminteri, and De Niro does a good job of doing just enough to make it feel like we’re in the room with the characters while letting Palminteri’s writing do the rest.

**Impression:**  
This would probably be a sleeper Saturday hit if I had seen it when I was a kid. You know, one of those movies that’s on tbs after you come home from baseball or tutoring that you say you want to watch for an excuse not to do homework, but really ends up being good enough that you’ll carve out time to watch it whenever it’s on next. No? Just me?

But seriously, contrived plot and poorly aged race relations dynamics aside (asking a lot here, on second read), the movie really does have a lot of heart. A good chunk of the appeal in De Niro’s ability to capture levity and ambience during the downtime scenes; it is fundamentally a movie about a neighborhood and a kid growing up, and I have a soft spot for bildungsromans anyways. But the performances by Palminteri and De Niro during their heart-to-hearts with Calogero and the New York gangster core of the plot are also well executed as dramatic  counterbalances. I think it’s hard to say there’s anything socially redeeming at all for the movie, but it is a sincere and honest approach to the gangster movie rather than the slick or gritty types that these films tend to lean towards. Overall, I think it’s a good December 15th-20th, week before Christmas type of movie you can watch while wrapping up work or chores, the kind with good background noise and great moments littered throughout to look up and appreciate before going back to business.
